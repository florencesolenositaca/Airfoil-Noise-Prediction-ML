## Project Overview

Aircraft and drone development requires careful evaluation of aerodynamic performance, including the noise generated when airflow interacts with airfoil surfaces. However, repeated physical wind-tunnel experiments can be costly and time-consuming, potentially delaying prototype development and increasing research expenditures.

This project applies **machine learning regression techniques to predict airfoil self-noise** using historical NASA wind-tunnel experimental data involving a NACA 0012 airfoil.

By comparing multiple regression models, the project aims to identify the most reliable approach for estimating acoustic noise levels and determine which aerodynamic conditions and airfoil characteristics are most strongly associated with noise generation.

The goal is to support **data-driven aerodynamic design evaluation, reduced reliance on preliminary physical testing, and more efficient drone development processes.**

## Dataset Information

This project uses the *Airfoil Self-Noise* dataset, containing experimental measurements of aerodynamic noise generated by airflow interacting with a NACA 0012 airfoil.

- **Objective:** Predict scaled sound pressure level (SSPL)
- **Unit of Analysis:** Individual wind-tunnel experimental observations
- **Dataset Size:** 1,503 observations
- **Target Variable:** Scaled Sound Pressure Level (SSPL), measured in decibels (dB)
- **Predictor Variables:** Five aerodynamic and geometric characteristics

### Key Dataset Features

- **Frequency (Hz)** – Frequency of the measured acoustic response
- **Angle of Attack (°)** – Angle between the airfoil and incoming airflow
- **Chord Length (m)** – Distance between the airfoil's leading and trailing edges
- **Free-Stream Velocity (m/s)** – Speed of airflow approaching the airfoil
- **Suction-Side Displacement Thickness (m)** – Boundary-layer displacement thickness on the suction side of the airfoil

### Dataset Source & Citation

## Machine Learning Implementation

### Regression Models

Five regression models are considered in this project:

- **Ridge Regression** – Applies L2 regularization to reduce coefficient instability.
- **LASSO Regression** – Uses L1 regularization to shrink coefficients and support feature selection.
- **Elastic Net Regression** – Combines L1 and L2 regularization.
- **Decision Tree Regressor** – Captures nonlinear relationships through rule-based data partitioning.
- **Random Forest Regressor** – Combines multiple decision trees to improve predictive accuracy and generalization.

### Model Evaluation

Model performance is assessed using:

- **Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE)** – Measures prediction error while penalizing larger deviations.
- **Mean Absolute Error (MAE)** – Measures the average absolute difference between predicted and actual noise levels.
- **Coefficient of Determination (R²)** – Measures how much variation in airfoil noise is explained by the model.
- **Cross-Validation** – Evaluates model stability and generalization across multiple training folds.

The project aims to achieve an **R² score of at least 0.80 on the held-out testing dataset**.

### Predictive Analysis

- Identification of key aerodynamic and geometric factors associated with airfoil noise
- Comparison of regularized linear regression and nonlinear tree-based models
- Evaluation of predictive accuracy and model generalization
- Identification of operating conditions associated with elevated sound pressure levels
- Interpretation of model results to support preliminary airfoil design evaluation

## Project Significance

This project demonstrates how machine learning can support **aerospace engineering and aeroacoustic analysis** by transforming experimental wind-tunnel measurements into predictive insights.

By examining the relationships between aerodynamic conditions, airfoil geometry, and acoustic output, the project explores how regression models can assist engineers in evaluating potential noise behavior before conducting additional physical experiments.

The findings highlight the potential of machine learning to support **cost-efficient aerodynamic testing, informed design decisions, and more efficient drone research and development.**

Although the dataset focuses on controlled airfoil self-noise measurements rather than complete aircraft or drone noise, the resulting models can provide a foundation for further investigation and engineering validation.
